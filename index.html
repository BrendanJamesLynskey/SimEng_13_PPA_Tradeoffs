What PPA Is, and Who Trades It

PPA is power, performance and area: the three quantities every chip design is judged on. The simulation series in this GitHub measure performance and power carefully; this deck adds the third axis, and names the trade-offs between all three.

WhoDecidesTrades, for example
ArchitectureWhich units, how many, how much on-chip memory, which memory technology, which algorithms the hardware is built forMore SRAM cuts off-chip traffic (performance, energy) and costs area
Micro-architecturePipelining, banking, datapath widths, the clock target, how units share ports and wiresA deeper pipeline raises the clock and costs registers (area, power)
Physical designFloorplan, cell libraries and threshold voltages, SRAM bitcell choice, wire layers, power gridLow-leakage cells cut static power and are slower
  • The three cannot be optimised separately. Power depends on the clock, which performance wants high. A bigger die has longer wires, which are slower and burn more energy per bit. On-chip memory saves off-chip energy and costs leakage and area. Each decision moves at least two axes.
  • Area matters because it is cost. Wafers are bought by the square millimetre, and yield falls as dies grow (slide 06), so a design twice the size costs more than twice as much.
  • Early decisions matter most. Architecture fixes most of the PPA envelope before any RTL exists, which is why an architect needs a simulator that reports all three axes, not just latency.
02

Performance: Latency and Throughput

Performance is two different quantities, and a design can win one while losing the other:

  • Latency: the time one task takes (one bootstrap, one token, one request), reported as a distribution with its tail, not an average.
  • Throughput: tasks per second when the machine is kept busy. Little's law links the two: concurrency = throughput × latency.
  • Batching, pipelining and more units in parallel raise throughput and often raise latency; the metrics users feel (time to first token, time per output token, goodput under an SLO) are the ones a product is judged on.

The metrics, their statistics and how to attribute them to hot-spots are covered in InfSim 06; this deck does not repeat them. Two points matter here:

  • A PPA comparison needs one performance number per design, chosen for the product: bootstraps per second for an FHE accelerator, tokens per second at a latency SLO for an LLM server.
  • The bound tells you which resource to buy. A memory-bound design gains nothing from more compute (slide 11 measures this): see bound attribution and the roofline.
03

Power: Dynamic, Static, DVFS and Dark Silicon

The two kinds of power

Dynamic: Pdyn = α C V² f, for activity factor α (the fraction of the capacitance C switched each cycle), supply voltage V and clock f. Static (leakage): Pstatic = V · Ileak, drawn whenever the circuit is powered, busy or not.

  • DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling) lowers f and V together. Over the range where f rises roughly with V, dynamic power grows roughly as f³ and energy per operation as V², so running slower saves energy per task, until static power, charged for the longer run time, eats the saving. InfSim 07 models DVFS and power caps; FHESim 05 shows a dynamic power manager against worst-case clocking.
  • TDP (thermal design power) is the heat the package and cooling can remove. A design whose units could together draw more must throttle: power becomes a limit on performance (a "power-bound" verdict in the FHE simulator).
  • Dark silicon. Since supply voltage stopped scaling with feature size (the end of Dennard scaling), each new node gives more transistors than the power budget can switch at once; part of the chip must be idle or slowed at any moment (Esmaeilzadeh et al., ISCA 2011). Specialised accelerators are one response: area that is dark most of the time but efficient when lit.
  • Energy is not power. Power limits the package; energy per task sets battery life and the electricity bill. A faster design at higher power can use less energy per task ("race to idle") when static power dominates.
  • Leakage is chosen, not given. CACTI models the same 512 MiB scratchpad leaking 0.14 W / 179 W with low-standby-power against high-performance transistors (22 nm; CACTI card). Large memories use low-leakage cells and give up speed.

Area: What Sets It

Four things take the silicon: SRAM bitcells (with their decoders and sense amplifiers), logic (here modular multipliers, which dominate FHE datapaths), I/O and PHYs (the analogue interfaces to HBM, PCIe, die-to-die links) and wiring, with the repeaters and pipeline registers long wires need. Estimating Area Before Layout

Before RTL, area comes from analytical models; after RTL, from synthesis; only layout gives the real number. Three pre-RTL estimators are explained, with their accuracy, on SimEng 12's cards: CACTI (SRAM and caches), McPAT (processors) and Accelergy and Timeloop (accelerators, from component tables). FHE_Accelerator_Sim's area model (ppa.py) is built the same way, from three sources:

  1. Units: ARK's per-unit areas (above) divided by its unit counts: about 0.007 mm² per NTT butterfly and 0.002 mm² per multiply-add lane, at 7 nm, wiring included. Scaling linearly to other counts is the illustrative part: wiring grows faster than the units it joins.
  2. SRAM: CACTI 7, built and run here, gives the shape of area against capacity for banked scratchpads (22 nm is its smallest node). One factor scales it to 7 nm so that 512 MiB matches ARK's scratchpad; BTS's 512 MB agrees within 3%.
  3. Everything else: HBM PHYs per stack, and register files plus on-chip network as ARK's fraction of the rest.

06

Why Area Is Cost: Dies per Wafer and Yield

Three formulas

Dies per wafer (wafer diameter d, die area A): π(d/2)²/A − πd/√(2A); the second term is the partial dies lost round the edge. Poisson yield: Y = e−A·D0 for defect density D0, if defects land independently and uniformly. Murphy yield (Murphy, 1964): Y = ((1 − e−A·D0) / (A·D0))², for a defect density that varies across the wafer. Cost per good die = wafer price / (dies per wafer × Y).

  • Cost grows faster than area. With the illustrative D0 = 0.1 per cm² and $10,000 per wafer, a good square millimetre costs 2.54x as much on an 858 mm² die as on a 100 mm² one (results.md §24).
  • The two models agree for small dies and part for large ones, because Murphy's varying density leaves some large dies defect-free. Raise D0 to see them diverge; at 0.5 per cm² Murphy's yield is 1.52× Poisson's for the FHE die. Which one a foundry's data fits is an empirical question.
  • The reticle sets a hard limit: a lithography field is 26 × 33 mm (858 mm²), so no single die can be larger. Past that, or well before it for cost, designs split into chiplets (slide 11).

Composite Metrics: perf/W, perf/mm², EDP and TCO

Combining axes into one number makes designs comparable, and each combination hides something. With perf = tasks per second, t = time per task and E = energy per task:

MetricFormulaRewardsHides
perf/W(1/t) / (E/t) = 1/EEnergy per task, and nothing elseSpeed: a slow design with the same energy scores the same
perf/mm²1 / (t · A)Throughput per unit of silicon (cost)Energy, and yield (a mm² on a big die costs more)
perf/$(1/t) / cost per good dieThroughput per unit of manufacturing costRunning cost, and everything but silicon
EDPE · tEnergy and speed equallyArea
ED²PE · t²Speed more than energy; roughly independent of supply voltage where f rises with V (E ∝ V², t ∝ 1/V), so it compares designs, not operating pointsArea
TCOpurchase cost amortised over the service life + energy × electricity price × datacentre overheadThe money an operator actually spendsNothing, but it needs prices you rarely have pre-silicon
  • Which product optimises which: a phone SoC lives on energy per task and area (perf/W, perf/$); a datacentre accelerator on throughput per TCO dollar, of which power is a large share; a supercomputer node on performance under a power cap; an edge device on cost and standby power.
  • perf/W equals 1/energy. The FHE simulator's default design scores 0.88 bootstraps per joule with the baseline algorithm, which is just 1 / 1139 mJ. Quoting both as if they were separate wins counts the same thing twice.
  • For energy per token and the energy–delay curve in LLM serving, see InfSim 07.
08

Pareto Fronts: Why There Is No Single Best Design

Design A dominates design B if A is no worse on every axis and better on at least one. The Pareto front is the set of designs nothing dominates. Every design off the front can be improved for free; every design on it is a different compromise, and choosing among them needs the product's priorities, not more simulation.

  • Adding an axis adds designs to the front. Two designs with equal latency and energy are both on a latency–energy front; the area axis separates them. In the FHE simulator's baseline scratchpad sweep, latency and energy both fall as SRAM grows, so on those two axes the largest scratchpads win outright; with area as a third axis, every size up to 2 GiB is on the front (slide 10). Its 36-design sweep without area has a single optimum (FHESim 05).
  • Finding the front is cheap once the points exist: search.pareto_nd compares every pair (O(n²), fine for hundreds of designs). Finding the points is the expensive part, which is why smarter experiments (bounds, bisection, surrogates) matter.

The Other Trade-offs, Named

The simulation series make several trade-offs without calling them that. Each is a front with two axes and no single best point:

Trade-offThe leverWhat it showsWhere
Latency against throughputBatch sizeBigger batches fill the hardware and raise throughput; every request in the batch waits longerInfSim 03 · batching, InfSim 05 · results
Time to first token against time per output tokenDisaggregating prefill and decodeSeparate pools stop prefill bursts stalling decode, at the cost of moving the KV cache and of hardware that cannot be sharedInfSim 05 · the interference problem, the idea
Precision against energyENOB of an optical engine's convertersEach extra bit doubles converter energy; too few bits forces narrower digits and more passesFHESim 04 · the precision tax, conversion energy
Memory against computeMin-KS, seeded keys, on-the-fly plaintextsRegenerating data on chip trades HBM traffic for multiplications: on the memory-rich ARK-class design a bootstrap falls from 13.94 ms to 7.19 ms; on the NTT-starved design it rises from 17.46 ms to 26.65 msFHESim 02 · algorithmic levers, FHESim 05 · acceleration techniques
Fidelity against simulation speedThe model's level of abstractionEach rung down the fidelity ladder is slower and more exact; loosely timed SystemC models trade timing error for speed through the quantumInfSim 01 · the fidelity ladder, InfSim 08 · choose the abstraction, SimEng 03 · the quantum trade-off
  • The same lever can move a design either way. The memory–compute row is the sharpest example in the series: the techniques that make the ARK-class design fast make the NTT-starved design slow, because they spend the resource it lacks.
  • Simulation itself is a PPA problem: a faster, less exact model is a different point on a fidelity–speed front, and the right choice depends on the question.

Pitfalls

  • Optimising one axis. The best perf/W in the baseline sweep (1.04 per joule) belongs to the 2 and 4 GiB designs, which no reticle can print. Report all three axes, or a composite that matches the product, and say which.
  • Illustrative coefficients presented as truth. The area model's units are one published design's breakdown scaled linearly, its SRAM is a 22 nm CACTI curve scaled by one factor, and its optical areas are placeholders. It is good for ratios, trends and crossovers between designs built from the same assumptions, and wrong as an absolute mm². The explorer's density factors exist to show how far conclusions move when the assumptions do.
  • Ignoring yield. Pricing area linearly undercounts large dies: at the illustrative D0, a good mm² on a reticle-sized die costs 2.54x one on a 100 mm² die.
  • Comparing designs at different nodes without scaling. ARK (418.3 mm² at 7 nm) and CraterLake (472.3 mm² at 12/14 nm) look similar in size (ARK's comparison table); at one node they would not be. Scale to a common node, or compare within one.
  • Counting the same thing twice. perf/W is 1/energy per task; perf/mm² and perf/$ both reward small dies. A table of five metrics may hold two independent facts.
  • Forgetting what the model leaves out: here HBM stacks, packaging, test and the system around the chip, none of which the silicon cost includes.
14

What to Take Away

  • Power, performance and area are traded together at every level from architecture to layout; a simulator that reports only latency answers a third of the question.
  • Area is cost, and cost grows faster than area through dies per wafer and yield, up to the reticle, past which the design must be split into chiplets.
  • Pick the composite metric that matches the product, and know what it hides: perf/W ignores speed, perf/mm² ignores energy, EDP and ED²P ignore area.
  • Pareto fronts replace "the best design" with the set worth choosing between; an extra axis can turn a single optimum into a front.
  • Buy the bound resource. On a memory-bound FHE design, SRAM returned 3.74 ms per 100 mm² and compute 0.21; and the algorithm changes which resource is bound.
  • Label every coefficient with its node and source, and say which are illustrative. The conclusions here are about shapes and crossovers, not absolute square millimetres.
